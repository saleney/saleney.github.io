# SALENE'S INTERNET
## Creative Direction & Design Constitution

Version 1.0

This document defines the creative direction, interaction philosophy,
visual principles, writing standards, and product instincts behind
Salene's websites.

Read this document before designing, redesigning, or substantially
modifying any project.

This is not a rigid component library.

It is a creative constitution.

Individual projects may have completely different aesthetics,
emotions, audiences, and interactions.

They should not look identical.

They should feel like they came from the same brain.


============================================================
01. THE BIG IDEA
============================================================

Salene's websites are not primarily websites.

They are:

• tiny interactive worlds
• digital toys
• useful little tools
• jokes taken unnecessarily seriously
• experiments
• personal artifacts
• learning experiences
• museum exhibits
• invitations to play
• manifestations of "wait, can I make that?"

The internet is a creative medium.

Treat it accordingly.

Do not begin by asking:

"What should a website like this look like?"

Begin by asking:

"What would make THIS PARTICULAR IDEA delightful to experience?"


============================================================
02. THE CREATIVE NORTH STAR
============================================================

The desired reaction is not:

"This is a very polished website."

The desired reactions are closer to:

"Wait, what is this?"

"I have to click that."

"HAHAHAHA."

"Wait, do it again."

"Who made this?"

"Of course Salene made this."

"This is weirdly delightful."

"I can't believe someone actually made this."

"I want to show someone."

Polish matters.

But authorship matters more.


============================================================
03. THE INTERNET SHOULD FEEL PLAYABLE
============================================================

These projects should encourage curiosity.

Whenever possible, visitors should interact rather than merely consume.

Prefer:

CLICK
↓
RESPONSE
↓
DELIGHT
↓
CURIOSITY
↓
CLICK AGAIN

over:

READ
↓
READ
↓
SCROLL
↓
READ
↓
UNDERSTAND
↓
FIND BUTTON
↓
CLICK

The user should learn the experience by experiencing it.

Do not write three paragraphs explaining something that one good
interaction can demonstrate.


============================================================
04. THE TWO SECOND RULE
============================================================

Within approximately two seconds of opening an interactive project,
the visitor should understand what they can interact with.

They do NOT need to understand everything.

Mystery is allowed.

Confusion about what to click is not.

Ask:

"What is the first thing I want the visitor to touch?"

Make that visually obvious.

The primary interaction should dominate over secondary information.


============================================================
05. THE DOPAMINE RULE
============================================================

Reduce the distance between curiosity and payoff.

The visitor should be able to get to the interesting part quickly.

Optimize for:

curiosity → action → response

Avoid unnecessary:

• introductions
• setup screens
• confirmations
• onboarding
• configuration
• explanatory copy
• intermediate buttons
• navigation decisions

Every additional interaction should justify itself.

If the user selects something and the next action is obvious,
consider advancing automatically.

If one tap can reasonably accomplish something, do not require three.


============================================================
06. LESS IS MORE, ESPECIALLY WITH WORDS
============================================================

Interactive websites are not instruction manuals.

Every sentence must earn its place.

Before adding copy, ask:

"Would the user understand this without the sentence?"

If yes:

DELETE THE SENTENCE.

Prefer:

UNHINGED 8 BALL

[ ASK THE BALL ]

over:

Welcome to Unhinged 8 Ball, an interactive experience where users
can submit questions and receive unpredictable answers.

Never explain the joke before the user experiences the joke.

Avoid:

• introductory paragraphs
• feature descriptions
• obvious instructions
• redundant subtitles
• marketing copy
• generic inspirational language
• explanations of interactions that are already intuitive

Use short copy with personality.

A five-word sentence that sounds unmistakably human is often more
valuable than a polished paragraph.


============================================================
07. THE IDEA IS THE UNHINGED PART
============================================================

"Unhinged" does NOT mean visually chaotic.

The weirdness should primarily come from:

• the premise
• the behavior
• the interaction
• the result
• the writing
• the unexpected specificity of the idea

Examples:

UNHINGED 8 BALL is inherently unhinged.

SHOULD I TEXT MY EX? is inherently unhinged.

BAD IDEA GENERATOR is inherently unhinged.

EMERGENCY BRAIN is inherently funny and specific.

They do not need visual chaos to prove it.

In fact:

RESTRAINED DESIGN
+
RIDICULOUSLY SPECIFIC PREMISE

is often funnier.

Do not translate "unhinged" into:

• visual clutter
• random colors
• excessive animation
• ten fonts
• neon everything
• decorative chaos
• illegibility

Controlled weirdness is stronger than random weirdness.


============================================================
08. SPECIFICITY IS PERSONALITY
============================================================

Generic is the enemy.

Specificity creates authorship.

Prefer details that could only belong to this project.

Avoid choices that could be copied unchanged into 10,000 websites.

Ask:

"Could this sentence/button/layout exist on somebody else's portfolio
without modification?"

If yes, question it.

Specificity can appear through:

• language
• interaction
• timing
• tiny jokes
• unusual labels
• strange outcomes
• unexpected transitions
• project-specific visual motifs
• easter eggs
• contextual details

Personality does not require decoration.

Sometimes one extremely specific sentence does more than an entire
illustration system.


============================================================
09. PASSION PROJECT ENERGY
============================================================

These websites should feel like somebody made them because she
genuinely wanted them to exist.

Not because:

• a stakeholder requested them
• a growth team approved them
• a template suggested them
• a portfolio needed another case study
• the market demanded them

Preserve the energy of:

"I had an idea and apparently nobody could stop me."

The work can be sophisticated without becoming sterile.

Do not polish away the evidence that a human being cared about it.


============================================================
10. AUTHORED DESIGN
============================================================

Favor evidence of authorship.

Good authored details include:

• unexpected but intentional spacing
• asymmetrical compositions
• one element breaking the grid
• unusual scale relationships
• project-specific typography moments
• tiny annotations
• marginal notes
• peculiar button labels
• easter eggs
• secret interactions
• visual callbacks
• unusual loading states
• custom error messages
• contextual empty states
• intentional surprises

These details should feel discovered rather than mass-produced.

Do not add randomness merely to simulate personality.

AUTHORED ≠ RANDOM.

Every unusual decision should have some relationship to:

• the premise
• the emotion
• the interaction
• the joke
• the story


============================================================
11. CONTROLLED IMPERFECTION
============================================================

Do not automatically make everything:

• symmetrical
• evenly spaced
• identically sized
• perfectly centered
• componentized into matching cards

Perfect repetition can feel machine-generated.

Allow controlled irregularity.

For example:

One project title might intentionally be enormous.

One annotation might sit slightly outside the expected grid.

One button might behave differently because it is THE button.

One object might be weirdly tiny because that is funny.

One element may unexpectedly occupy the whole viewport.

But irregularity must remain intentional.

Never sacrifice usability merely to appear handmade.


============================================================
12. ANTI-AI AESTHETIC
============================================================

Do not default to the statistical average of "modern website."

Actively avoid common AI-generated design tropes unless the project
specifically justifies them.

DO NOT AUTOMATICALLY ADD:

• purple-blue gradients
• gradient mesh backgrounds
• glowing blobs
• glassmorphism
• excessive drop shadows
• giant rounded cards
• endless card grids
• pill labels everywhere
• decorative sparkles
• generic floating shapes
• feature sections
• testimonial sections
• fake statistics
• unnecessary dashboards
• huge generic marketing headlines
• generic illustrations
• generic hero + features + CTA + footer structure
• meaningless animations
• "modern tech" decoration
• generic Apple-style emoji icons

Never add something merely because websites commonly contain it.


============================================================
13. THE CARD TEST
============================================================

AI loves putting everything in rectangles.

Resist this.

Before putting content inside a card, ask:

"Why does this need a container?"

Cards are appropriate when they communicate:

• grouping
• selection
• containment
• hierarchy
• interaction

Cards are NOT necessary merely because several pieces of content exist.

Prefer:

typography
spacing
alignment
composition

before adding another rectangle.

If the page starts looking like:

[ CARD ] [ CARD ] [ CARD ]
[ CARD ] [ CARD ] [ CARD ]

stop and reconsider the information architecture.


============================================================
14. ICONOGRAPHY
============================================================

Never use platform-dependent emoji as interface icons.

No Apple-style emoji buttons.

No Unicode emoji substituted for intentional interface design.

Emoji may appear in actual written content when the writing genuinely
calls for them, but they should not function as the project's default
icon system.

When icons are necessary:

Prefer:

• simple SVGs
• cohesive stroke weights
• project-appropriate visual language
• clear silhouettes
• restrained detail
• accessible implementation

Icons should improve recognition.

Do not add icons merely because empty space exists.

If text communicates the action better:

USE TEXT.


============================================================
15. WHIMSY COMES FROM BEHAVIOR
============================================================

Prioritize interactive whimsy over decorative whimsy.

Prefer:

• something responding unexpectedly to a click
• an object reacting to the visitor
• playful state changes
• unusual microcopy
• hidden interactions
• tiny discoveries
• surprising motion
• contextual reactions
• easter eggs
• playful randomness
• meaningful sound where appropriate

over:

• another blob
• another gradient
• another decorative illustration
• another sparkle
• another floating icon

Make the website DO something delightful.


============================================================
16. INTERACTIONS SHOULD REINFORCE THE PREMISE
============================================================

Interactions are not decoration.

Ask:

"What would this particular object naturally do?"

An 8 ball should behave like an 8 ball.

A coin should feel like flipping a coin.

A museum should reward wandering.

An emergency tool should respond immediately.

A bad idea generator should feel suspiciously eager to enable bad ideas.

The interaction itself should tell part of the story.


============================================================
17. DO NOT OVER-ANIMATE
============================================================

Motion should have a job.

Good reasons for animation:

• communicate state
• provide feedback
• reinforce physical metaphor
• direct attention
• create anticipation
• deliver a punchline
• create delight

Bad reason:

"Animation looks cool."

Avoid simultaneous competing motion.

One excellent interaction is better than six mediocre animations.

Respect prefers-reduced-motion.

The experience should remain coherent and delightful without motion.


============================================================
18. PROJECTS ARE ROOMS, NOT TEMPLATES
============================================================

Salene's websites belong to the same universe.

They should NOT all have identical layouts.

Think:

ONE MUSEUM
MANY ROOMS.

Each room has its own atmosphere.

Examples:

UNHINGED 8 BALL
mysterious
dramatic
slightly cursed
minimal

EMERGENCY BRAIN
warm
immediate
calm
playful
useful

AN AUTOBIOGRAPHY TOLD THROUGH LOVE
intimate
archival
sentimental
exploratory

听不懂
chaotic
curious
linguistic
travel-infused

A LITTLE ADVENTURE
gentle
imaginative
childlike without being childish

Same creator.

Different emotional worlds.


============================================================
19. VISUAL CONTINUITY WITHOUT SAMENESS
============================================================

Projects can share sensibilities without sharing components.

Continuity can come from:

• confident typography
• restrained palettes
• concise writing
• interaction-first design
• deliberate whitespace
• project-specific humor
• editorial composition
• strong hierarchy
• controlled weirdness
• thoughtful mobile experiences

Do not copy layouts simply to create consistency.

Consistency of TASTE matters more than consistency of templates.


============================================================
20. TYPOGRAPHY
============================================================

Typography is architecture.

Use it to create:

• hierarchy
• rhythm
• tension
• humor
• emphasis
• pacing

Body text must remain highly readable.

Avoid defaulting automatically to:

giant sans-serif headline
+
neutral sans-serif body
+
gray supporting text
+
rounded CTA

Consider editorial typography where appropriate.

Use unusual scale intentionally.

Allow text itself to become a visual object when the project supports it.

Do not use novelty fonts for long body copy.

Do not sacrifice readability for personality.


============================================================
21. COLOR
============================================================

Color should feel chosen.

Do not use gradients as shorthand for:

creative
technology
AI
modern

Choose a restrained palette appropriate to each project's emotional
world.

A project may have:

• one dominant background
• one primary text color
• one or two supporting tones
• one memorable accent

This is not a rigid formula.

Use color functionally.

Maintain accessible contrast.

If removing a color makes the composition stronger:

remove it.


============================================================
22. WHITESPACE
============================================================

Whitespace is intentional.

Use space to:

• establish hierarchy
• create anticipation
• isolate important interactions
• make interfaces breathable
• create rhythm

Do not confuse whitespace with randomly enormous gaps.

Every large empty area should feel composed.

Avoid accidental blank areas caused by:

• arbitrary min-heights
• excessive margins
• rigid viewport sections
• poor responsive behavior

Space should feel designed.


============================================================
23. MOBILE IS NOT SMALL DESKTOP
============================================================

Mobile must be designed intentionally.

Do not merely stack the desktop layout vertically.

Ask:

• What does the user need first?
• What can disappear?
• What should become larger?
• What should become closer?
• What should automatically advance?
• Can this be used with one thumb?
• Does the interaction still feel delightful without hover?
• Is the important thing visible without unnecessary scrolling?

Mobile should preserve the project's personality.

Do not remove all whimsy merely to make the layout fit.


============================================================
24. THE TEN-SECOND UTILITY RULE
============================================================

For utility projects such as Emergency Brain:

assume the user may have only ten seconds.

Optimize for:

• immediate recognition
• minimal decisions
• minimal reading
• large touch targets
• automatic progression where appropriate
• actionable information first
• secondary explanation later

Utility should not mean boring.

Personality can remain.

But usefulness wins whenever personality interferes with speed.


============================================================
25. COPY VOICE
============================================================

Write like a human.

Prefer:

specific
conversational
confident
dry
playful
occasionally absurd
occasionally tender

Avoid:

corporate
marketing-heavy
over-explanatory
inspirational filler
generic UX copy
AI-polished prose

BANNED ENERGY:

"Discover..."
"Unlock..."
"Elevate..."
"Explore the possibilities..."
"Where creativity meets..."
"Designed to empower..."
"Seamless experience..."
"Your journey starts here..."

unless the project is intentionally making fun of those phrases.

Do not sand down strange language.

Strange language is often where the personality lives.


============================================================
26. BUTTON COPY
============================================================

Buttons are writing.

Avoid defaulting automatically to:

SUBMIT
CONTINUE
LEARN MORE
GET STARTED

when something more specific communicates the interaction.

Prefer:

FLIP THE COIN
ASK THE BALL
GIVE ME A BAD IDEA
JUST GIVE ME SOMETHING
ANOTHER IDEA

The user should know what the button will DO.

Button language can reinforce the premise.


============================================================
27. ERROR STATES ARE PART OF THE WORLD
============================================================

Do not automatically use generic:

"Something went wrong."

When appropriate, error states can belong to the project's voice.

However:

clarity comes first.

The user must still understand:

• what happened
• whether they caused it
• what they can do next

Personality should support comprehension, not obscure it.


============================================================
28. RANDOMNESS
============================================================

Randomness is a recurring creative material in Salene's projects.

Use it intentionally.

When randomizing:

• avoid immediate repetition where possible
• understand the probability model
• preserve meaningful constraints
• do not claim randomness when weighting exists
• ensure results fit the current context

Randomness should create replayability.

It should not create broken experiences.


============================================================
29. SOUND
============================================================

Sound may be used when it meaningfully enhances an interaction.

Never assume sound is welcome.

Avoid:

• unexpected loud audio
• essential information conveyed only through sound
• constant background audio without control

Sound should feel like a reward or physical response.

Provide appropriate control.


============================================================
30. EASTER EGGS
============================================================

Easter eggs are encouraged.

But they should remain Easter eggs.

They should not interfere with the primary experience.

Good Easter eggs:

• reward repeated interaction
• reference another project
• reveal hidden copy
• respond to unusual behavior
• reward curiosity

The best reaction is:

"WAIT. WAS THAT THERE THE WHOLE TIME?"


============================================================
31. ACCESSIBILITY IS NOT THE BORING PART
============================================================

Whimsy must remain usable.

Preserve:

• semantic HTML
• keyboard navigation
• visible focus states
• appropriate contrast
• touch target sizes
• readable text
• meaningful button labels
• reduced motion
• appropriate ARIA
• screen-reader comprehension

Do not create an inaccessible experience and call it experimental.

Constraints can inspire better creative decisions.


============================================================
32. PERFORMANCE
============================================================

Do not make a tiny joke website load like enterprise software.

Prefer simplicity.

Avoid unnecessary:

• libraries
• dependencies
• huge assets
• animation frameworks
• network requests

when native HTML/CSS/JavaScript can accomplish the experience.

Fast response is part of good interaction design.


============================================================
33. DO NOT ADD TECHNOLOGY FOR PRESTIGE
============================================================

Technology serves the idea.

The idea does not exist to justify technology.

Do not add:

• React
• databases
• authentication
• APIs
• backends
• frameworks
• state-management libraries

merely because they are available.

Add technical complexity when the experience actually requires it.

Ask:

"What capability does this unlock for the visitor?"

If there is no compelling answer:

do not add it.


============================================================
34. CONTENT ARCHITECTURE
============================================================

Organize content according to how visitors think, not according to
how the code happens to be structured.

Use human mental models.

Examples:

For Emergency Brain:

"What classroom moment am I experiencing?"

is more useful than:

"What type of pedagogical activity would you like?"

For the larger Salene website:

projects may eventually be organized by emotional or experiential
worlds rather than technical format.

Possible worlds include:

WHOLESOME
UNHINGED
PERSONAL

Taxonomy should serve exploration.


============================================================
35. HOMEPAGE PHILOSOPHY
============================================================

The homepage is an entrance to the museum.

It should not explain every exhibit.

Let project names, visual identity, and tiny hints create curiosity.

The homepage should encourage:

"I want to click that."

Avoid turning it into a résumé.

Avoid turning every project into:

thumbnail
+
project title
+
three-sentence case study
+
tech stack
+
VIEW PROJECT

unless there is a specific reason.

The portfolio itself should demonstrate the work.


============================================================
36. CREATIVE DIRECTOR MODE
============================================================

Before implementing anything, separate:

THE IDEA
What is the premise?

THE EMOTION
What should this feel like?

THE ACTION
What should the visitor do?

THE PAYOFF
Why will they want to do it?

THE VISUAL LANGUAGE
What world does this belong to?

THE RESTRAINT
What can be removed?

THE SURPRISE
What makes this specific?

Only then decide:

HTML
CSS
JavaScript
components
animation
layout

Technology comes after creative direction.


============================================================
37. THE ONE MEMORABLE THING RULE
============================================================

Every project should ideally have one thing someone remembers.

Not twenty.

Ask:

"If someone uses this once and tells a friend about it tomorrow,
what do I hope they describe?"

That thing might be:

• the coin
• the fog
• a ridiculous result
• an interaction
• a sentence
• a sound
• a visual transformation
• an unexpectedly tender moment

Design around that memory.


============================================================
38. THE SCREENSHOT TEST
============================================================

A screenshot should look intentional.

But a screenshot should NOT communicate the entire value of an
interactive project.

If somebody can fully experience the website from a screenshot,
the interaction may not be doing enough.

These are websites.

Use the medium.


============================================================
39. THE FRIEND TEST
============================================================

Imagine sending the link to a friend with no explanation.

Can they figure out what to do?

Do they interact?

Do they get the joke?

Do they want to send it to somebody else?

If the website requires a paragraph from Salene explaining how to
experience it:

the interface needs work.


============================================================
40. THE DELETE TEST
============================================================

At the end of every design pass, ask:

"What can I delete?"

Try removing:

• one sentence
• one container
• one label
• one decorative element
• one interaction step

If nothing important is lost:

leave it deleted.

Addition is not the only form of design.

Editing is design.


============================================================
41. AGENT INSTRUCTIONS
============================================================

When an AI coding agent modifies one of these projects:

1. Inspect the existing implementation first.

2. Identify what is already distinctive.

3. Preserve working behavior unless explicitly instructed otherwise.

4. Preserve personality.

5. Make the smallest coherent change that satisfies the request.

6. Do not redesign unrelated areas.

7. Do not automatically "modernize" the interface.

8. Do not add dependencies unnecessarily.

9. Do not replace authored copy with generic UX language.

10. Do not add emoji as interface icons.

11. Do not introduce generic AI aesthetics.

12. Test mobile intentionally.

13. Preserve accessibility.

14. Respect reduced-motion preferences.

15. Test existing interactions after changes.

16. Inspect the diff for unrelated modifications.

17. Do not refactor merely because refactoring is possible.

18. Explain significant design or architectural decisions after
implementation.

When uncertain:

PRESERVE before REPLACE.

REMOVE before ADD.

SIMPLIFY before DECORATE.


============================================================
42. THE FINAL SALENE TEST
============================================================

Before considering a design finished, ask:

INTERACTION

□ Can I tell what to click almost immediately?
□ Is the interesting part reached quickly?
□ Does interaction produce satisfying feedback?
□ Would I want to click again?
□ Are there unnecessary steps?

COPY

□ Can any sentence be deleted?
□ Am I explaining the joke?
□ Does this sound like Salene?
□ Is any language generic?
□ Are button labels specific?

VISUAL DESIGN

□ Could this be mistaken for an AI-generated landing page?
□ Are there too many cards?
□ Too many pills?
□ Too many containers?
□ Unnecessary gradients?
□ Generic emoji?
□ Is everything suspiciously symmetrical?
□ Does whitespace feel intentional?

AUTHORSHIP

□ What choice could only belong to this project?
□ Where is the evidence a particular person made this?
□ Is there something worth discovering?
□ Does the premise determine the design?

MOBILE

□ Does this actually work beautifully on a phone?
□ Can primary interactions work one-handed?
□ Is anything dependent on hover?
□ Is unnecessary scrolling slowing the experience?

ACCESSIBILITY

□ Keyboard usable?
□ Focus visible?
□ Contrast sufficient?
□ Touch targets sufficient?
□ Reduced motion respected?
□ Screen-reader meaning intact?

TECHNOLOGY

□ Did we add anything unnecessary?
□ Is the simplest implementation sufficient?
□ Does every technical choice support the experience?

FINAL QUESTION

Would Salene send this URL to a friend and say:

"LOOK WHAT I FUCKING MADE"

If yes:

COMMIT.
PUSH.
DEPLOY.

If no:

figure out why before adding more shit.
